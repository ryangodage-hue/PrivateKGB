# Study Plan: *Paṭicca Samuppāda Dhammaya* (පටිච්ච සමුප්පාද ධර්මය)

**Ven. Kaṭukurunde Ñāṇananda Thera**, Pahan Kanuwa sermons 183–202. Library edition, 20 sermons, about 555 pages.

This plan was written after reading every sermon in full. It covers:

1. What the book is trying to do.
2. The order to study it in, and why.
3. A guide for each sermon: what it teaches, the key suttas and similes, why it sits where it does, questions to reflect on, and a practice task.
4. Cross-reading maps, a timetable, and some cautions.

---

## 1. What this book is, and why the order matters

Read this before you start.

These are **spoken sermons, not chapters of a textbook**. They were given month by month on poya days to a lay village audience. That is why every sermon opens by recalling earlier ones, why similes come back again and again (the whirlpool, the dog on the plank, Narcissus, the cricket match, the tug-of-war, the five fingers), and why some sermons match the festival calendar (Vesak in S9, Poson in S10, Esala in S11, the end of the rains retreat in S15).

The whole series argues for **one central thesis**, and everything else hangs on it:

> The twelve links are *illustrations* of a single principle, **idappaccayatā** ("this being, that is; this ceasing, that ceases"). At the heart of that principle is the **mutual dependence of consciousness (viññāṇa) and name-and-form (nāmarūpa)**. This is the "whirlpool" (*vaṭṭa*) of the Mahānidāna Sutta. Ignorance means not seeing this pair as a pair that depends on each other. Taking it instead as "two" (self and world, here and there) is what spins saṃsāra. When the whirlpool stops, the result is **Nibbāna, experienced here and now** (*diṭṭheva dhamme*). Nibbāna is the cessation of the six sense-bases and the cessation of existence (*bhava-nirodha*). It is not a place after death.

Six supporting ideas recur through the book:

- **Nāma means only five factors:** feeling, perception, intention, contact and attention (*vedanā, saññā, cetanā, phassa, manasikāra*). The author teaches them as the five fingers. Consciousness is not one of them, and rūpa is the perceived form ("name-only form"), not physicists' matter.
- **Dependent origination is the Middle** (Kaccānagotta Sutta). It avoids both "everything is" and "nothing is", and it is a deeper middle than the one between sensual indulgence and self-torment.
- **Language and conceiving (*maññanā*) are part of the problem.** The arahant uses words "without grasping" them.
- **Saṅkhāra has two different senses.** In dependent origination it means the bodily, verbal and mental formations. In the five aggregates it means intention.
- **The commentaries' three-lives model breaks idappaccayatā.** The author holds that all twelve links can be seen in the present.
- **Yoniso manasikāra is attention to the source, that is, to contact.** It is the "crown witness" that turns the case.

Because each sermon assumes the ones before it, **the book's own order is the best study order.** The plan below keeps that order but groups the 20 sermons into **six parts**, each with a clear purpose and a short review at the end. A few cross-readings are added where a later sermon finishes an earlier topic.

## 3. Guide for each sermon

Page numbers are from the library edition. For each sermon: **(a)** read the opening verses and the author's gloss first; **(b)** read the sermon straight through once, as a talk; **(c)** in a second sitting, look up the one or two key suttas (the footnotes give Buddha Jayanti Tripiṭaka references); **(d)** do the reflection and the practice task.

### PART I: FOUNDATIONS (S1–S4)

#### S1. *Yadā have pātubhavanti dhammā* (Udāna, Bodhi Suttas), pp. 1–27
- **Teaches:**
  - The Buddha's three watches of the night: arising order, cessation order, then both together.
  - The **key distinction between the principle (idappaccayatā) and its twelve illustrations.**
  - The 16 doubts (Sabbāsava Sutta) that are dispelled.
  - "*Avijjāya tveva asesavirāganirodhā*": when ignorance ceases, everything ceases at once. This is not a three-lives story.
  - The first appearance of viññāṇa ⇄ nāmarūpa and of the five-factor definition of *nāma*.
- **Similes:** dog on the plank (barking at its own reflection); the cricket and cinema "worlds"; the Zen tug-of-war (Ajith and Sumith); Uggasena the acrobat ("*muñca pure, muñca pacchato…*").
- **Why first:** It sets the rule of the whole book: *don't memorise twelve links; see the principle.* Assaji's verse "*ye dhammā hetuppabhavā…*" is read as pointing to the law, not to ignorance as a first cause.
- **Reflect:** Why would someone who can recite the twelve links forwards and backwards still miss dependent origination? What does "this being, that is" add that the list alone does not?
- **Practice:** Each day, pick one ordinary experience (a sound, an irritation) and say silently: "*This arose dependent on that; when that ceases, this ceases.*"

#### S2. *Kuto sarā nivattanti* (Sara Sutta), pp. 28–55
- **Teaches:**
  - The **whirlpool simile in full**: a current turning against the river creates a vortex, an "abyss" below and a pull of attraction above.
  - Vipassī's discovery that "*consciousness turns back from name-and-form; it goes no further*".
  - The **five-finger mnemonic** (little finger = feeling … thumb = attention); attention as the **"crown witness"**.
  - The Kevaddha verse "*viññāṇaṃ anidassanaṃ…*": consciousness that no longer reflects anything.
- **Similes:** Mahosadha's gem in the pond (a reflection, not a jewel); Narcissus; the Tissa Metteyya verse on the seamstress.
- **Why here:** S1 named the pair. S2 gives you the **picture** (whirlpool) and the **tool** (five fingers) that the author uses in every later sermon.
- **Reflect:** In the gem-in-the-pond story, what corresponds to draining the pond, and what to looking up at the tree? Which of your five "fingers" usually starts trouble?
- **Practice:** Learn the five fingers by heart. During one sitting, each time the mind wanders, ask: "Which finger moved first?"

#### S3. *Eka mūlaṃ dvirāvaṭṭaṃ* (Ekamūla Sutta), pp. 56–81
- **Teaches:**
  - The riddle verse decoded from the suttas. The *root is contact*; the *abyss is painful bodily feeling*; the ocean is the six sense-bases.
  - **The Mahānidāna Sutta**: designation contact vs. impact contact (*adhivacana-* vs *paṭigha-samphassa*); consciousness descending into the womb; "*ettāvatā vaṭṭaṃ vaṭṭati … nāmarūpaṃ saha viññāṇena*".
  - Brahmajāla: all 62 views "*tadapi phassapaccayā*" (that too is conditioned by contact).
  - Titthāyatana links dependent origination to the Four Noble Truths.
- **Similes:** stones colliding (no quarrel) vs. pedestrians colliding (a quarrel); the carpenter's leaning planks; the "mud stain" in the womb.
- **Why here:** It gives the **scriptural base** (the Mahānidāna) for the thesis of S1–S2, and shows why the Mahānidāna can omit ignorance and formations without being "incomplete".
- **Reflect:** What is the difference between being struck by an object and being struck by a *name*? Why is "this is as far as language, logic and wisdom go" such a strong claim?
- **Practice:** When a painful bodily feeling arises, note "*abyss*", and look for the swing towards pleasure that usually follows (*dvirāvaṭṭa*, turning two ways).

#### S4. *Anto jaṭā bahi jaṭā* (Jaṭā Sutta), pp. 82–108
- **Teaches:**
  - The inner tangle is nāma ⇄ rūpa; the outer tangle is nāmarūpa ⇄ viññāṇa.
  - The Cetanā Sutta: consciousness that is *established* (rebirth) vs. *unestablished* (freedom).
  - Sāti's wrong view: consciousness as a traveller from life to life. Instead, consciousness is named by its fuel, like fire.
  - The **Naḷakalāpa Sutta: two sheaves of reeds** leaning on each other.
- **Similes:** the monkey swinging through branches; the near-death "shadow" falling on a womb; the hollow reeds (no core).
- **Why here:** It closes Part I by giving the **first account of rebirth without a soul** and the image that sums up the series: two sheaves of reeds (the author notes it was put on the cover of the first published volume).
- **Reflect:** If consciousness is "named by its condition", what exactly is reborn? What happens to the second sheaf when the first is pulled away?
- **Practice:** Notice intentions, plans and underlying tendencies (*ceteti, pakappeti, anuseti*) as "footholds". At night, review: where did my mind take a foothold today?

> **Part I review (1 week):** Draw the whirlpool and the two sheaves from memory. Explain the five fingers and the Mahānidāna "ettāvatā" passage to someone else. If you can't, re-read S2 and S3.

### PART II: HOW THE WHIRLPOOL BUILDS A "WORLD" (S5–S8)

#### S5. *Akkheyyasaññino sattā* (Samiddhi Sutta), pp. 109–136
- **Teaches:**
  - Beings perceive in terms of what can be named and stand on it; the **limits of linguistic convention**.
  - "*Voharavepakkaṃ saññaṃ vadāmi*": perception ripens into speech.
  - The arahant speaks "by convention only".
  - Sandha's "thoroughbred jhāna" (meditating on nothing at all).
  - Lakuṇṭaka Bhaddiya: "*chinnaṃ vaṭṭaṃ na vaṭṭati*" (the cut whirlpool no longer spins).
  - Upasīva's flame that "*goes out and cannot be reckoned*".
  - The first reading of the Kaccānagotta Sutta.
- **Similes:** the dying rich man reborn by his chair; the magic kettle; Aśvaghoṣa's lamp.
- **Why here:** Part I showed *how* the vortex works. S5 shows its **product, a world of names**, and answers the charge that this teaching is annihilationism.
- **Reflect:** How does grammar ("I / you / he"; "here / there") quietly assume a self? What exactly "goes out" in the simile of the flame?
- **Practice:** For one day, catch yourself thinking "I am …" and add "*by convention*".

#### S6. *Jāti-maraṇa saṃsāraṃ* (Dvayatānupassanā: ignorance), pp. 137–164
- **Teaches:**
  - Saṃsāra is **the journey of ignorance itself**: moving between "thisness" and "otherwiseness" (*itthabhāva / aññathābhāva*).
  - The **Kaccānagotta Sutta in full: dependent origination is the Middle.**
  - Bāhiya: "*in the seen, just the seen*".
  - "*Nissitassa calitaṃ*": the one who leans, wavers.
  - Lokavolokana: "*whatever they conceive it as, it becomes otherwise*".
  - Vepacitti's bonds and the nine conceits (*maññitas*); Puṇṇa's mirror.
- **Similes:** the snake-circle cartoon (two snakes swallowing each other); **Siyadoris's death** (everyone blames a different cause; the Buddha says "because he was born").
- **Why here:** It begins the **Dvayatānupassanā series** (contemplations in pairs), which structures much of the rest of the book. It also defines "the Middle" that every later sermon relies on.
- **Reflect:** Why is "everything exists / nothing exists" a trap even at an everyday level? In the Siyadoris story, which "cause" would you have given?
- **Practice:** Contemplate in pairs. For anything you cling to, note both "*this arises*" and "*this ceases*" in the same moment.

#### S7. *Anattani attamāniṃ* (the 15th dyad), pp. 165–190
- **Teaches:**
  - The world takes what is not-self as self, "settled in name-and-form".
  - The **Mūlapariyāya Sutta (MN 1)**, which the author calls the "ABC of the Dhamma": 24 concepts and **three attitudes** (the worldling conceives; the trainee is told "*let him not conceive*"; the arahant does not conceive). Even "Nibbāna" can be conceived.
  - **Ānanda's arahantship**, reached on the eve of the First Council in the gap between postures.
  - **Khemaka's** lingering scent of "I am".
- **Similes:** the blindfolded walker who reaches the wall marked "Nibbāna" and must turn back; the lotus scent; the washed cloth in a scent box.
- **Why here:** S6 named conceiving; S7 shows it operating **on every concept, even the goal**. This prepares you for the redefinition of Nibbāna in S8.
- **Reflect:** Why did Ānanda's very eagerness for arahantship get in his way? Where in your own practice is "Nibbāna" turning into a "thing"?
- **Practice:** Use the three attitudes as a check on one object a day (earth, a person, "my meditation"): *perceiving → conceiving → delighting*.

#### S8. *Rūpā saddā rasā gandhā* (the 16th dyad), pp. 191–216
- **Teaches:**
  - **The cessation of the six sense-bases is Nibbāna.** Sources: the Kāmaguṇa Sutta (Ānanda: "*saḷāyatana-nirodhaṃ … sandhāya bhāsitaṃ*"); Udāna "*atthi … tadāyatanaṃ*"; Rohitassa ("in this fathom-long body … the world and its end"); Lokantagamana.
  - **Brahmajāla:** the fisherman's net, and all views caught in the mesh of contact.
  - Uṇṇābha: the chain of refuges (senses → mind → mindfulness → release → Nibbāna).
- **Similes:** the **gourd folktale** (the exorcist still drumming inside a gourd swallowed by a shark): all philosophers dance inside the "gourd" of the six sense-bases; "Siyadoris Nibbāna" (the after-death heaven of obituary notices).
- **Why here:** It is the **climax of Part II**. Once language, conceiving and the Middle are clear, the author can say plainly what Nibbāna is and is not.
- **Reflect:** If "the world" means the six sense-bases, what does "the end of the world" mean? Why would the commentaries say nothing about Rohitassa?
- **Practice:** In a quiet sitting, ask: *where is "the world" right now?* Look only at the six doors.

> **Part II review:** Write one paragraph each on: the Middle (Kaccānagotta); the three attitudes (Mūlapariyāya); Nibbāna as cessation of the six sense-bases. **Cross-read S14 (pp. 360–385) now if you want** (see §4), since it completes S8.

### PART III: THE MIDDLE IN ACTION (S9–S12)

#### S9. *Yo ubhante viditvāna* (Majjhe Sutta; Vesak), pp. 217–243
- **Teaches:**
  - **Six interpretations of "the two ends and the middle"**, with craving as the **seamstress** stitching the two ends together.
  - The Buddha's own reading: contact / arising of contact / cessation of contact. All six are valid meditation subjects.
  - Adhimutta Thera facing the bandits without fear.
  - The **insight knowledges** (rise and fall → dissolution → fear → disenchantment).
- **Similes:** the **sewing machine** (the lower thread secretly knots each stitch; "*a machine with no lower thread*" = the fruition attainment); walking a ridge holding a crumbling fence; the swordsman without a shield.
- **Why here:** It is the **bridge from theory to practice** and gives you six ready-made meditation subjects. The seamstress image returns in S17 and S20.
- **Reflect:** Which of the six pairs of "ends" is most alive in your life right now? What is the "middle" you keep stepping over?
- **Practice:** Choose **one** of the six interpretations and use it as your meditation subject for the whole week.

#### S10. *Lobhajaṃ dosajaṃ ceva* (Nidāna Sutta; Poson), pp. 244–270
- **Teaches:**
  - **Kamma**: its three roots; Nibbedhika ("*cetanāhaṃ … kammaṃ vadāmi*"; the source of kamma is contact).
  - The **Devadūta Sutta** (preached by Mahinda in Sri Lanka); Cūḷakammavibhaṅga.
  - **A full critique of the three-lives model**: in the Vibhaṅga Sutta, saṅkhāra means breath, applied and sustained thought, and perception-feeling, *not* past kamma; viññāṇa is the six kinds of consciousness, *not* "rebirth consciousness".
  - **Two right views**: the mundane view of kamma ownership (*kammassakatā*) and the supramundane view (the Four Noble Truths).
  - *Kusala* (skill) vs. *puñña* (merit).
- **Why here:** After hearing the commentaries challenged, a reader naturally asks: "*Does this deny kamma and rebirth?*" S10 answers **no**. Keep the mundane right view, and do not mix it up with the supramundane view.
- **Reflect:** Why does the author insist that rejecting "this world, the next world, mother, father" is wrong view? How is *kusala* more than *puñña*?
- **Practice:** Use the five daily reflections (Ṭhāna Sutta: "*kammassakomhi …*" — I am the owner of my actions). Before one deliberate act each day, notice the intention (the "middle finger").

#### S11. *Ye ca rūpūpagā sattā* (Santatara Sutta; Esala), pp. 272–299
- **Teaches:**
  - The **truth of cessation**.
  - **The Poṭṭhapāda Sutta in depth**: perceptions arise and cease *through training* (*sikkhā ekā saññā uppajjanti …*). The jhānas form a staircase up to the **peak of perception** (*saññagga*), then "*not intending, not forming*" → touching cessation.
  - The three acquisitions of self.
  - "*lokasamaññā … yāhi tathāgato voharati aparāmasaṃ*": the Tathāgata uses the world's expressions without grasping them.
- **Similes:** **milk → curd → butter → ghee** (each name is valid only at its own time); the man in love with an unknown beauty queen; the ladder to an unknown palace.
- **Why here:** It shows dependent origination as a **graded training** (*anupubba-sikkhā*), not only an insight, and it links language (S5) to meditation.
- **Reflect:** Why can't the same thing be called "milk" and "ghee" at once? How does that apply to "self" in past, present and future?
- **Practice:** In each sitting, notice which perception ends when the mind settles (for example, sound-perception fades). Note "*a perception has ceased through training*".

#### S12. *Cirassaṃ vata passāmi* (Oghataraṇa Sutta), pp. 302–327
- **Teaches:**
  - "*Not standing still, not struggling, I crossed the flood.*"
  - The four floods: sensuality (hedonists vs. Jain ascetics); existence (the Itivuttaka: those who **hold back** and those who **overshoot**); views (Kaccānagotta again); ignorance (the influxes are its origin).
  - **The Sabbāsava Sutta's seven methods.**
  - Kappa's island: "*akiñcanaṃ anādānaṃ*" (owning nothing, grasping nothing).
- **Similes:** chasing vs. fleeing one's shadow; the swimmer who must give way to the current; "decay itself decayed, death itself died".
- **Why here:** It **completes Part III** by turning the Middle into a full **practice toolkit** (seven methods).
- **Reflect:** In your practice, are you "standing still" or "struggling"? Which of the seven methods do you neglect?
- **Practice:** Make a one-page plan using the seven methods (seeing, restraining, using, enduring, avoiding, removing, developing) for one defilement you know well.

> **Part III review:** Summarise the seven methods and the six "middles". **Re-read S1–S2 quickly.** You will now notice much more in them.

### PART IV: THE MACHINERY UP CLOSE (S13–S16)

#### S13. *Yaṃ kiñci dukkhaṃ sambhoti* (the saṅkhāra dyad), pp. 330–357
- **Teaches:**
  - **The two senses of saṅkhāra.**
    - (A) In dependent origination: bodily, verbal and mental formations (breath; applied and sustained thought; perception and feeling). This is the "backstage rehearsal".
    - (B) In the aggregates: the six groups of intention.
  - The six stillings (*passaddhi*) up to the cessation of perception and feeling.
  - The Khajjanīya Sutta ("*saṅkhataṃ abhisaṅkharonti*": formations are what form the formed).
  - "*Anicca vata saṅkhārā*" (impermanent indeed are formations): the deepest verse in the series, now reduced to a funeral chant.
  - Conditioned vs. unconditioned.
- **Similes:** the iceberg; the actors' make-up room; Pacetana's chariot wheel; the spinning top that seems to stand still (*āneñja* formations, which keep beings in the Brahma worlds).
- **Why here:** It begins the **walk through the dyads** and clears up the most common confusion in commentarial readings of dependent origination.
- **Reflect:** Which kind of saṅkhāra is in play when you breathe, when you think before speaking, and when you plan revenge? Why can't Nibbāna be "a thing" (*asaṅkhata-dhātu* as a place)?
- **Practice:** Ānāpānasati with the aim of *calming the bodily formation* (*passambhayaṃ kāyasaṅkhāraṃ*).

#### S14. *Idaṃ jātu vedagū* (Udaka Sutta), pp. 360–385
- **Teaches:**
  - The six sense-bases.
  - **Sabba Sutta** ("the All"); Samiddhi ("where there is no eye … there is no world").
  - **Mahākoṭṭhita**: the cessation of the six contact-bases = **the cessation of proliferation (*papañca*)**.
  - Moliya Phagguna ("*who touches?*" is the wrong question).
  - **Mahāsaḷāyatanika Sutta**: accumulation vs. dismantling, the path factors arising together, calm and insight yoked.
  - The perception of impermanence (*aniccasaññā*).
- **Similes:** the **tortoise** pulling in its limbs while the jackal waits; black and white oxen yoked by one rope (the fetter is the rope, not the oxen); the boil and its root.
- **Why here:** It is the technical **sequel to S8**. It lays the groundwork for the contact and feeling sermons that follow.
- **Reflect:** Why is "is there anything left after the six bases cease?" the wrong kind of question? Where is the "rope" between your eye and what you look at?
- **Practice:** Guarding the senses: not grasping at signs and details (*na nimittaggāhī*). Take one walk a day doing this.

#### S15. *Tesaṃ phassaparetānaṃ* (the contact dyad; end of the rains retreat), pp. 388–414: **meditation workshop**
- **Teaches:**
  - **The secret of contact.** Contact arises when the *product* (eye-consciousness) is counted as a *third thing* beside its two conditions.
  - *Yoniso manasikāra* = attending at the point of contact.
  - **The Indriyabhāvanā Sutta**: how fast to return to equanimity at each sense door.
- **Similes:**
  - **Table tennis against Māra** (return the ball at the point of impact with "*anicca!*"); the duel without a shield.
  - The Indriyabhāvanā speeds: **blink, finger-snap, water rolling off a lotus leaf, spitting, bending an arm, drops on a red-hot pan**.
  - The cicada's "rrrr", heard as a heap of sounds; Paduma the "scent thief"; honey on a razor; the flayed cow.
  - **The computer** (desire = mouse, attention = cursor, contact = menu); Rathavinīta's relay of chariots; the **four-runner relay race**.
- **Why here:** The author says it is a practical sermon. After 14 sermons of foundation you are ready to use these similes **on the cushion**.
- **Reflect:** At which sense door is your "return" slowest? What would "returning the ball at impact" look like for a harsh word?
- **Practice:** **Do this one for a full week.** Each sitting, pick one sense door and train the "speed of return" given in the Indriyabhāvanā Sutta.

#### S16. *Sukhaṃ vā yadi vā dukkhaṃ* (the feeling dyad), pp. 418–442: **meditation workshop**
- **Teaches:**
  - **Cūḷavedalla**: pleasant feeling is pleasant while it lasts and painful when it changes (and the reverse for painful feeling).
  - Where the underlying tendencies do *not* lie: no lust in the first jhāna, no aversion in grief born of renunciation, no ignorance in the fourth jhāna.
  - The chain of counterparts that ends in Nibbāna.
  - **Phassamūlaka**: two sticks rubbed together make heat; separate them and it ceases.
  - **Nandakovāda**: the lamp; the tree and its shadow; the **butcher separating hide from flesh** (the sinews are delight-and-lust, the knife is noble wisdom). The nuns were "not yet full" on the 14th and became stream-enterers on the 15th.
  - **Godhika**: "*appatiṭṭhitena viññāṇena*" (with consciousness unestablished).
- **Similes:** the seesaw (Salla Sutta); deer tracks on rock (neutral feeling); bubbles in the rain; the horse-race photo finish.
- **Why here:** It follows contact naturally (contact → feeling) and gives the most **direct instructions for working with pain** in the book.
- **Reflect:** Why does neutral feeling hide ignorance? What is the "knife" and what are the "sinews" in your own experience of pain?
- **Practice:**
  - Lengthen sitting time **by five minutes at a time**.
  - Make the fourfold determination ("*kāmaṃ taco ca nhāru ca …*": let only skin, sinew and bone remain).
  - Stay with a sensation until its *compact perception* breaks into a "heap of bubbles".
  - **Read the cautions in §6 first.**

> **Part IV review:** For two weeks, use S15 and S16 as your main meditation manuals. Keep a log of "speed of return" and "bubbles".

### PART V: THE FAR END OF THE CHAIN (S17–S19)

#### S17. *Taṇhādutiyo puriso* (the craving dyad), pp. 446–471
- **Teaches:**
  - Why one sutta says the origin of suffering is *craving* and another says it is *the twelve links*. The answer: **craving is the mother, ignorance the father.** There is no need to look for a "root of ignorance".
  - **Mahāsatipaṭṭhāna**: the ten places where craving arises and settles, including *craving for craving itself*.
  - "*Aññathābhāvī bhavasatto loko*": the moment something becomes "a thing", it starts to change, so rebirth (*ponobhavikā*) happens **moment by moment**, not only at death.
  - **Kutūhalasālā**: a flame carried by the wind; craving as fuel between bodies; why the antarābhava doctrine is a misreading.
  - **Is wanting Nibbāna craving?** Bhikkhunī Sutta ("*taṇhaṃ nissāya taṇhā pahātabbā*": with craving, abandon craving) and Uṇṇābha (the desire to reach the park ends on arrival).
- **Similes:** the toy that becomes "a thing" once bought; the hare caught in a snare; the tree regrowing from an intact root; the overhanging ridge (*bhavanetti*); railway-carriage hooks turned the other way; the mirage posts.
- **Why here:** Contact → feeling → **craving**: the dyads continue in order. It also settles a puzzle many students carry for years (desire for the goal).
- **Reflect:** Where do you find "craving for craving" (enjoying the wanting itself)? How is wholesome *chanda* different from worldly goals that spawn new goals?
- **Practice:** Each evening, note one moment where a "thing" was born (*itthabhāva*) and one where it changed (*aññathābhāva*).

#### S18. *Upādāna paccayā bhavo* (the grasping dyad), pp. 474–499
- **Teaches:**
  - The four kinds of grasping. Grasping is neither the same as nor separate from the aggregates; it is **desire-and-lust for them**.
  - Bhava Sutta: "*kammaṃ khettaṃ, viññāṇaṃ bījaṃ, taṇhā sineho*" (kamma the field, consciousness the seed, craving the moisture).
  - **Upanisa Sutta**: the "transcendental" dependent origination (suffering → faith → gladness → … → knowledge of destruction). *Upanisā* means "supporting condition", not "necessary condition".
  - Nibbedhika: suffering leads either to **bewilderment or to a search** (Paṭācārā, Kisāgotamī).
  - **Ajāta Sutta**: "*there is an unborn …*" = bhava-nirodha known in fruition here and now, not a separate world.
  - The Mahānidāna "*ettāvatā …*" again; Muni Sutta (the sage "is not reckoned"); the four-cornered logic and the unanswered questions.
  - **Parinibbuta means in this life** (Cūḷasaccaka: "*parinibbuto so bhagavā parinibbānāya dhammaṃ deseti*" — the Blessed One, fully quenched, teaches for full quenching).
- **Similes:** rain on a mountain filling streams, rivers and the ocean; field, seed and water.
- **Why here:** It is the **philosophical peak of Part V**. It pulls together the whirlpool (S2–S4), language and logic (S5), and Nibbāna (S8, S14).
- **Reflect:** In your own life, has suffering led more often to bewilderment or to search? Why would doubt (= ignorance) remain if freedom could only be confirmed after death?
- **Practice:** Recite the Upanisa sequence and identify honestly where you stand on it today.

#### S19. *Yaṃ kiñci dukkhaṃ sambhoti* (the nutriment dyad), pp. 502–526
- **Teaches:**
  - **The four nutriments**: food, contact, mental intention, consciousness. All are rooted in craving.
  - **Puttamaṃsa Sutta** similes: **the son's flesh** (food); **the flayed cow** (contact); **the charcoal pit** (intention); **the robber struck 300 times a day** (consciousness), updated by the author to the "ad-strokes" of TV and neon signs.
  - The author criticises the commentary's sentimental retelling that blunts the shock.
  - The middle way with food: reflection (*paccavekkhaṇa*) → elements → repulsiveness → **emptiness**.
  - **Atthirāga Sutta**: with lust, consciousness "lands and grows" like a **painter drawing a figure on a wall**. Without lust it is like **sunlight through a peaked house with nowhere to land** (*appatiṭṭhita*) = *anidassana viññāṇa*. Painting on empty space (Kakacūpama).
- **Why here:** It is the final link of the chain in the dyad series. It gives the **clearest account of how rebirth happens** (consciousness "painting" name-and-form) and how it stops.
- **Reflect:** Which nutriment do you "eat" most hungrily: food, contact, intention or consciousness (screens)? What would it mean for the light to find no wall?
- **Practice:** Before each meal, recite the food reflection (*paṭisaṅkhā yoniso …*) and add the element reflection: "*mere elements, without a being, empty*".

> **Part V review:** Read S17–S19 alongside S4 (Cetanā Sutta) and S9 (Kutūhalasālā). Write your own explanation of **rebirth without a soul**.

### PART VI: CAPSTONE (S20)

#### S20. *Ye me pubbe viyākaṃsu* (Hemaka Sutta), pp. 530–555
- **Teaches:**
  - The **solution in the present**: Hemaka rejects others' "*it was so, it will be so*" talk. The Buddha's answer is the removal of desire-and-lust for the seen, heard, sensed and cognised, *diṭṭheva dhamme* (here and now).
  - **The critique of the Visuddhimagga's 20-mode / three-lives model**, which, the author argues, turns dependent origination into "it was so" talk.
  - How the terms *hetu / paccaya* (cause / condition) drifted in later texts (Nettippakaraṇa, Paṭṭhāna, Paṭisambhidāmagga), while in the suttas they are synonyms.
  - **The Āṇi Sutta prophecy**: monks of the future who will not listen to the deep suttas on emptiness.
  - The stream-enterer as one who glimpses the cessation of formations.
  - **History of the series**: the Nibbāna sermons at Mītirigala, requested by Ven. Mātara Śrī Ñāṇārāma.
  - **Yoniso manasikāra as "the seed of wisdom present in everyone"**; the Sañcetanā Sutta ("*so kāyo na hoti …*" — that body does not exist).
- **Similes (all revisited):** cinema, gambling or tug-of-war, magic show, cricket, love (Narcissus): each is a little *bhava* with its own language. Also the **banana trunk split lengthwise** (no core).
- **Why last:** It **sums up the whole book** and openly states its most controversial claims. Without Parts I–V it reads as polemic; after them it reads as a conclusion.
- **Reflect:** Can you now explain, in your own words and with a simile, why the twelve links can be seen *in this present moment*? What has your "crown witness" (attention) started to testify?
- **Practice:** Take up "*yaṃ kiñci samudayadhammaṃ sabbaṃ taṃ nirodhadhammaṃ*" (whatever has the nature to arise has the nature to cease) as a standing reflection, and **go back to S1**.

## 6. Reading cautions

1. **This book argues against the standard commentaries.** The author rejects:
   - the Visuddhimagga three-lives model;
   - the commentarial definition of *nāma* (as four aggregates including consciousness);
   - the *antarābhava* (intermediate existence);
   - the idea of the unconditioned as a "place".

   These are serious, sutta-based arguments, but they are a **minority position** within Theravāda. The best way to study them is to **read the suttas he cites yourself** (the footnotes give the references) and judge for yourself.
2. **Kamma and rebirth are not rejected.** S10 insists that the mundane right view (kamma, this world and the next) must be kept. The critique is aimed at a *model* of dependent origination, not at kamma.
3. **S16, the Godhika story,** is about letting go of grasping at the moment of death. The author warns explicitly against reading it as approval of suicide. If you are working with pain as the sermon suggests, do it gradually (five minutes at a time), and take any real medical condition to a doctor.
4. **S19, the son's flesh,** is a shock simile about craving for *all* nutriments. The author says plainly that it is **not a teaching about meat versus vegetarian diets**.
5. **The Sinhala is spoken and colloquial**, with village similes chosen for a lay audience (the author says so in S20). Take the similes as pointers, not as precise doctrine.
