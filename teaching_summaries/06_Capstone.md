# Part VI: Capstone, the Solution in the Present

*Teaching summary of Sermon 20 (pp. 530–555), the final sermon of the series*

> **Builds on everything:** S20 reviews every major simile and argument in the book, states its most controversial claims openly, and tells the story of how the series came about.
> **Read it last**, then return to Part I. You will see how much more you now understand.

## Core teaching 1: The Buddha's answer is in the present (Hemaka Sutta)

The young brahmin Hemaka says to the Buddha (Pārāyana Vagga):

> "*Those who explained things to me before, outside Gotama's teaching, all said '**it was so, it will be so**'. All of that was hearsay, **all of it increased speculation**. I took no delight in it. **You, sage, teach me the Dhamma that destroys craving**, knowing which and living mindfully I may cross over attachment in the world.*"

The Buddha replies:

> "*Here, Hemaka, in things **seen, heard, sensed and cognised** that are dear: **the removal of desire-and-lust** for them is **the imperishable state of Nibbāna**. Those who understand this, mindful, **fully quenched here and now**, are always at peace; they have crossed over attachment in the world.*"

**The point:** other teachers tried to solve saṃsāra by **arguing about the past and the future**. The Buddha's solution is **in the present**: in the seen, heard, sensed and cognised, here and now. This is what the Dhamma's qualities **"visible here and now, timeless, come and see"** mean.

### Takeaway
> **Dependent arising is not a theory about past and future lives. It is the structure of experience right now, and that is where it is solved.**

## Core teaching 2: Why the three-lives model is a problem (summary of the whole book's critique)

### The Visuddhimagga model
Buddhaghosa divides the twelve links over three lives, and adds a **20-mode** scheme:

> "*Five causes in the past, five fruits now; five causes now, five fruits in the future.*"

- **Past causes:** ignorance, formations, craving, grasping, existence
- **Present fruits:** consciousness, name-and-form, six sense-bases, contact, feeling
- **Present causes:** (the same five as the past causes)
- **Future fruits:** (the same five as the present fruits)

He also speaks of **three rounds** (*vaṭṭa*): defilement, kamma, result.

### The author's response
1. **It breaks idappaccayatā.** The Buddha said the relationship between any two links is "***thus, not otherwise, not other than thus***" (*tathatā, avitathatā, anaññathatā*). Splitting links across lives, and **regrouping** them, breaks that direct "this being, that is" bond.
2. **It turns the Dhamma into "it was so, it will be so"**: exactly what Hemaka rejected.
3. **The real "round" (vaṭṭa)** is the one in the **Mahānidāna Sutta**: "***the round goes round … name-and-form together with consciousness***". That is the whirlpool, and it contains **all** birth, decay and death within it.
4. **The two sheaves of reeds** (Naḷakalāpa): pull consciousness and name-and-form falls, and with it the six sense-bases, contact, feeling, craving, grasping, **all at once**. That is why the Dhamma is **timeless**: there is nothing to spread over three times.

> **Remember:** the author does **not** deny rebirth (see Parts III and V). His target is a **model** that, he argues, hides how dependent arising can be seen and ended **now**.

## Core teaching 3: The dyad (dvayatā), and why "two" is the problem

The **contemplation of pairs** has run through the book (the Dvayatānupassanā Sutta). In S20 the author draws the thread together.

- **Arising / cessation** (*samudaya / vaya*): "*contemplating the nature of arising … of passing away*" (Satipaṭṭhāna).
- **Internal / external** (*ajjhatta / bahiddhā*): "*the body internally … externally*" (Satipaṭṭhāna).
- **The Dvaya Sutta 1:** "*I will teach you **the dyad**: eye and forms, ear and sounds … mind and mind-objects. Anyone who says he'll show another dyad is just talking.*"
- **The Dvaya Sutta 2:** "***Dependent on a dyad, consciousness arises***: *dependent on eye and forms arises eye-consciousness. The eye is impermanent, changing, becoming otherwise; forms are too. **This dyad is moving, tottering, impermanent.***"
- **The Bālapaṇḍita Sutta (SN):** "*For the fool, hindered by ignorance and fettered by craving, **this body has come to be**. So there is **this body and name-and-form externally: this is a dyad**. Dependent on the dyad there is contact …*"

**The problem of "two" is solved by impermanence.** When both sides are seen as moving and changing, there is nothing solid on either side to stand on.

> The author notes that the Theravāda tradition never made "**dvayatā**" (duality) a technical term, though the suttas point to its importance again and again.

## Core teaching 4: How the meaning of "cause" drifted

### In the suttas, hetu = paccaya
In the Mahāpuṇṇama Sutta a monk asks: "*What is the **cause** (hetu) and what is the **condition** (paccaya) for the manifestation of the form aggregate … the consciousness aggregate?*"

The Buddha gives **one answer** to both:
- **Form:** the four great elements are the cause and condition.
- **Feeling, perception, formations:** **contact** is the cause and condition.
- **Consciousness:** **name-and-form** is the cause and condition.

So in the suttas, "*cause*" and "*condition*" are **synonyms**.

### In later texts, they came apart

| Text | What it did |
|---|---|
| **Nettippakaraṇa** | "*Two things produce: cause and condition. **The cause is specific, the condition is common**.*" A sprout: the **seed** is the cause; **soil and water** are conditions. "*Own-nature is the cause, other-nature is the condition; internal is the cause, external is the condition.*" |
| **Paṭṭhāna** | Lists **24 conditions**. "*Cause*" (hetu) became **just one of the 24**. "*Mutual condition*" (*aññamañña paccaya*) also became just one of them, not the deep principle of the Mahānidāna. |
| **Paṭisambhidāmagga** | "*Ignorance is the cause, formations are cause-arisen; formations are the cause, consciousness is cause-arisen …*": each link a cause of the next. |

### What the "cause" really is
Recall Assaji's verse (Part I): "*Of things that arise from a cause, the Tathāgata has told **the cause***." The author argues the **cause is the principle itself, idappaccayatā**, not ignorance or any single link. All twelve links are **"dependently arisen things"**.

The Paccaya Sutta separates the two clearly:
- **Dependent arising (paṭicca samuppāda)** = the law: "*Whether Tathāgatas arise or not, **this element stands**, this stability of the Dhamma, this fixed course of the Dhamma, **specific conditionality**.*"
- **Dependently arisen things (paṭicca samuppannā dhammā)** = ignorance, formations … decay-and-death: "*impermanent, conditioned, dependently arisen*".

> **Why it matters:** if you split "cause" into "own-nature" and "other-nature", you are back to the old debate: "*Did I make it, or did someone else?*" The Buddha avoided that debate by showing the **structure**.

## Core teaching 5: The twelve links answer the old dilemmas by showing the structure

People in the Buddha's time were caught in **dilemmas** (*either / or*):
- "*Everything exists*" or "*nothing exists*" (Kaccānagotta, Part II)
- "*Suffering is self-made*" or "*made by another*" or "both" or "neither" (Acela Kassapa Sutta)

**The Buddha didn't pick a side.** Instead he laid out the twelve links, which **show the structure of the problem**:

1. In the **dark background of ignorance**,
2. **formations** (preparations) occur,
3. through which **consciousness ⇄ name-and-form** spin as a whirlpool;
4. this splits into the **six sense-bases**: eye *and* form, ear *and* sound (the dyad);
5. then **contact, feeling, craving, grasping, existence, birth**: the whole story.

Answering that way **dissolves** the dilemma rather than choosing one horn of it.

## Core teaching 6: Every "world" runs on the twelve links (the final review of similes)

S20 brings back the book's similes as a series of "**little worlds**" inside the sense-sphere world. **Each one shows all twelve links working, with the whirlpool at the centre.**

| World | How it works |
|---|---|
| **The cinema world** | It starts only when the hall goes **dark** (ignorance). The actors' rehearsal and make-up (formations) make it convincing. A whole world of joy and sorrow appears. **When the lights come on**, all the colour is washed away at once. People buy tickets **to be deluded**. |
| **The magic world** | It lasts only until you **see the magician's threads**. Glimpse them from backstage, and the magic world is over. |
| **The gambling world** (the Zen tug-of-war) | Two friends, Ajith and Sumith, play a game in which the loser must die. One is ready to die for the other. At the climax the teacher **sweeps the pieces off the board**, and the "world" of winning and losing ends. |
| **The cricket world** | Once the players divide into **two sides**, the equipment comes alive, the players get special names, and people even kill themselves over a loss. Birth, death, sorrow and lamentation are all there. |
| **The love world** (Narcissus) | "*The world is suffering, but where we two are is bliss.*" When the soap-bubble bursts: suicide or murder. Narcissus died for love of his own reflection. |

**Each world has its own language**, its own "*pathway of designation, of expression, of concepts*" (Mahānidāna). Inside it, there is birth, decay and death. **Seeing how it is made is how you step out of it.**

> **Consciousness asks name-and-form "who are you?"; name-and-form says "ask consciousness".** The pair is **not two and not one**: it is **a magic show** (*māyā*).

## Core teaching 7: Stilling the formations, the real way out

To be free of existence, **all three formations must be stilled**:
- **bodily formation** = in-and-out breath;
- **verbal formation** = applied and sustained thought;
- **mental formation** = perception and feeling.

These are the "**bottom floor**" (the hidden base). Nibbāna is "***the stilling of all formations***" (*sabbasaṅkhārasamatha*). But they don't still by force: **they still when the light of wisdom comes**, like the cinema lights coming on.

Then consciousness becomes **non-manifesting** (*anidassana*). The author stresses that these all name **the same thing**:
- cessation of the six sense-bases;
- cessation of consciousness;
- cessation of name-and-form.

All of them are realised **in the present**.

## Core teaching 8: Why the deep suttas were sidelined

### The Āṇi Sutta prophecy
The Buddha foretold:

> "*In the future there will be monks who, when **the discourses spoken by the Tathāgata, deep, deep in meaning, supramundane, connected with emptiness**, are being recited, **will not listen, will not lend an ear, will not apply their minds to understand them**, and will not think them worth learning or mastering.*"

The author believes this happened. **Very deep suttas**, such as the **Mūlapariyāya, Madhupiṇḍika, Mahānidāna and Mahāpadāna**, were labelled as **"conventional teachings" (*vohāra desanā*)** and pushed aside. Even the commentators and Western scholars found them hard. Yet people like **Bāhiya** became arahants from a single short teaching.

### Learning, practice, realisation
The Dhamma has three parts: **learning, practice, realisation**. Over time, **realisation declined first**, then **practice**, until mainly **learning** remained. Scholars took centre stage, and commentaries and sub-commentaries multiplied ("*cartloads of books*"). Within a hundred years of the Buddha's passing there were already **18 schools**: **the Dhamma is that deep**.

### The author's tone
He admits these views "**may look revolutionary**", and says they must be stated "**whatever criticism comes**". He calls his work an effort to clear "**a path overgrown for centuries**".

> **For the learner:** take this as a **reason to read the suttas yourself**. It is not a reason to dismiss the commentarial tradition without study.

## Core teaching 9: The stream-enterer and the banana trunk

### What does the stream-enterer see?
Not just "good morals". The stream-enterer sees:

> ***Yaṃ kiñci samudayadhammaṃ, sabbaṃ taṃ nirodhadhammaṃ.***
> "*Whatever has the nature to arise, all that has the nature to cease.*"

With the light of wisdom, they glimpse, **for a moment, the cessation of formations**, of consciousness. Seeing arising and ceasing in the five aggregates, the mind **turns toward Nibbāna and can never be turned back**. The fetters break gradually after that, but the direction is fixed.

### The banana trunk
> To see that a banana trunk has **no core**, do you have to peel it layer by layer? No: **cut it lengthwise with a sharp sword**, and the inner structure shows at once that there's no heartwood.

Seeing **this life** clearly is enough: "*if you understand this birth, the past and future are the same.*"

### Anyone can do it
Even **Suppabuddha the leper**, the poorest of the poor, saw the Dhamma on hearing the Buddha. What is needed is **practice** (virtue, concentration, wisdom), not certificates or memorised lists.

## Core teaching 10: Wise attention, the seed of wisdom in everyone

The book ends where it began, with the **five fingers** and the **crown witness**.

- **Don't be discouraged** ("*I'm just an ordinary person, I don't have the wisdom*"). The Buddha pointed to **the seed of wisdom present in everyone: wise attention**.
- **Attention (the thumb)** was "pardoned" from the gang of five name-factors and made a **crown witness**. For aeons it acted **unwisely**, helping the others commit "the crime of saṃsāra". Now it must **tell the truth**.
- Telling the truth is what **satipaṭṭhāna** means: "*contemplating the body as body, feeling as feeling …*": **feeling as feeling, perception as perception, intention as intention**.
- In the end even the biggest thief is caught: "***All things come into being through attention***" (*manasikārasambhavā sabbe dhammā*). The mind finds "a thing" only through attention.

### What happens when attention turns wise
1. The **four perversions are reversed**: perceptions of permanence, pleasure, beauty and self give way to perceptions of **impermanence, suffering, foulness and not-self**.
2. The **compact perception** that held "two ends" **melts** through contemplating impermanence.
3. **Heap perception** appears: things are seen as **a stream** of arising and ceasing.
4. That ends in **disenchantment** (*nibbidā*), **the door to Nibbāna**.

### Name-and-form is not physics
Nāma and rūpa are not to be explained like a physicist's atoms. **Rūpa is a perception of form**, known through name. It can't be seen with instruments, **only with wise attention**, supported by **virtue and concentration**.

### When ignorance ends (Sañcetanā Sutta)
> "*When there is a body, pleasure and pain arise internally because of bodily intention … when there is speech … when there is mind … **conditioned by ignorance**. With the remainderless fading and cessation of ignorance, **that body does not exist, that speech does not exist, that mind does not exist**; that field, that site, that base, that ground does not exist, conditioned by which pleasure and pain arise internally.*"

## The story behind the series

About twenty years earlier, at the **Mītirigala Nissaraṇa Vanaya** forest hermitage, meditating monks had questions about Nibbāna. The author's teacher, **Ven. Mātara Śrī Ñāṇārāma**, asked him in his own hut to give a series of sermons on Nibbāna so that they could be recorded.
- The author smiled and said that if he said what he had to say, he would be attacked.
- The next day his teacher **encouraged him** and said: "*Say in the sermon that I invited you.*"

That produced the **Nibbāna sermons** (*Nivane Niveema*, English: *Nibbāna – The Mind Stilled*). People who once looked at them "with squinting eyes", he says, now look with open eyes.

Later, some asked him to **write a book on dependent arising**. He felt it was easier to **preach** to the lay audience at **Pahan Kanuwa**, who had heard hundreds of his sermons and "*don't get up and leave when the Dhamma gets deep*". The forest-monk sermons used one set of similes; for this **village audience he used village similes**. That is no disrespect to the Dhamma; it is suiting the teaching to the listener.

He closes by dedicating the merit to his parents, teachers and supporters. He offers the series as **a garland woven from flowers picked from the deep suttas**, laid before the Dhamma itself.

## The whole book in ten sentences

1. Dependent arising is a **principle** ("this being, that is"); the twelve links are **illustrations** of it.
2. Its centre is **consciousness ⇄ name-and-form**, a **whirlpool**, two **sheaves of reeds**.
3. **Name** = feeling, perception, intention, contact, attention; **form** = form as perceived; consciousness is their partner.
4. **Ignorance** is not seeing this pair as mutually dependent; taking it as "**I**" and "**world**" starts everything.
5. **Language and conceiving** harden that split; the arahant uses words **without grasping**.
6. Dependent arising is **the Middle** between "everything exists" and "nothing exists".
7. **Contact** is the root and **craving** the seamstress; with wise attention at the point of contact, the stitch is not made.
8. **Kamma and rebirth are real**: consciousness lands where there is lust, like a **painter's picture**; without lust, like **light in a house with no wall**, it lands nowhere.
9. **Nibbāna** is the **cessation of the six sense-bases, of existence, of formations**, known **here and now**, not "Siyadoris Nibbāna".
10. The way there is **virtue, concentration and wisdom**, with **wise attention** as the seed that everyone already has.
