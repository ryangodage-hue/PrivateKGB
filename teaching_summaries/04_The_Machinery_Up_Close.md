# Part IV: The Machinery Up Close (the Practice Sermons)

*Teaching summary of Sermons 13–16 (pp. 330–442)*

> **Builds on Parts I–III:** the whirlpool, the five fingers, the Middle, Nibbāna as cessation of the six sense-bases, training as a staircase.
> **Part IV:** zooms in on **formations, the six sense-bases, contact and feeling**, and gives the book's most **hands-on meditation instructions**. The author calls S15 and S16 explicitly practical sermons.

From here on, many sermons take their opening verses from the **Dvayatānupassanā Sutta** (contemplation in pairs). Each one shows: "*All suffering arises because of X; with the cessation of X there is no suffering.*" Part IV takes X = **formations**, then the six sense-bases, **contact** and **feeling**.

## Core teaching 1: The two meanings of saṅkhāra (S13)

This is one of the **most useful clarifications in the book**, because the same word is used in two different places with two different meanings.

### (A) Saṅkhāra in dependent origination: the three "preparations"
The Buddha (Vibhaṅga Sutta), and the nun Dhammadinnā (Cūḷavedalla Sutta), define these. The word is **singular** in each case:

| Formation | What it is | Why called that |
|---|---|---|
| **Bodily formation** | **in-and-out breathing** | breath is bodily, bound up with the body |
| **Verbal formation** | **applied and sustained thought** (*vitakka-vicāra*) | first one thinks, *then* breaks into speech |
| **Mental formation** | **perception and feeling** | they are mental, bound up with the mind |

**The author's image is a theatre.** Ignorance is **the darkness of the hall**. These three formations are **the rehearsal and make-up behind the stage**: the preparation that makes the play possible. Consciousness ⇄ name-and-form is **the play on stage**. The **five aggregates of grasping are the result**.

**Iceberg simile:** nine-tenths is under water. Breathing seems harmless, and ordinary breathing doesn't send anyone to hell, **but the deep breath you take before hitting someone does**. The formations are the hidden base of all action.

### (B) Saṅkhāra in the five aggregates: intention
In the **aggregates** (form, feeling, perception, **formations**, consciousness), formations are the **six groups of intention**: intention about forms, sounds, smells, tastes, touches, mind-objects. Here the word is **plural**, and it is the **conditioned** product.

### The Khajjanīya Sutta: why they are called "formations"
> "*They **form the formed** (saṅkhatam abhisaṅkharonti), therefore they are called formations. They form form as form … feeling as feeling … **they even form formations as formations** …*"

Formations are what **make things "made"**. The same sutta defines **form** as "*what is **afflicted***" (by cold, heat, hunger, thirst, insects). The five aggregates are like **foam, a bubble, a mirage, a plantain trunk (no core), a magic show** (Pheṇapiṇḍūpama): deceptive at first glance, empty on close inspection.

### Stilling the formations step by step (Rahogata Sutta)
| Attainment | What is stilled |
|---|---|
| 1st jhāna | speech |
| 2nd jhāna | applied and sustained thought (verbal formation) |
| 3rd jhāna | rapture |
| 4th jhāna | **in-and-out breath** (bodily formation) |
| Cessation of perception and feeling | **perception and feeling** (mental formation) |
| Arahantship | lust, hate, delusion |

The pre-Buddhist yogis got as far as "neither-perception-nor-non-perception" and **made "nothing" into a "thing"** (→ the Brahma worlds). The Buddha's key was: "*What if I **neither intend nor form**?*" Fold down the middle finger (intention) and **touch cessation**: signless, undirected, empty.

### Momentum: the chariot wheel and the spinning top
- **Pacetana's chariot wheel:** a wheel made carelessly in six days, and another made carefully over six months. Pushed, each rolls "**as far as the momentum of its preparation (*abhisaṅkhāra*) carries it**", then falls. Volitional formations work like this.
- **The spinning top:** spun hard enough, a top **looks perfectly still** while it is actually spinning fast. **"Imperturbable" formations** (the formless jhānas) are like that: they **keep beings in the Brahma worlds for aeons**, still saṃsāra.

### The deepest verse, now a funeral chant
> ***Aniccā vata saṅkhārā, uppādavayadhammino;***
> ***uppajjitvā nirujjhanti, tesaṃ vūpasamo sukho.***
> "*Impermanent indeed are formations, of the nature to arise and pass away. Having arisen, they cease. **Their stilling is bliss.***"

Today we hear it **only at funerals**. The author says it is one of the deepest verses in the Dhamma: **Nibbāna is "the stilling of all formations"** (*sabbasaṅkhārasamatha*), the first phrase in the standard description of Nibbāna.

### Conditioned vs unconditioned
The conditioned has three marks: **arising, passing away, and change while it lasts** (AN 3). The unconditioned has **none of these**. But careful: the author warns against making the "unconditioned" into **a thing or a place** ("Siyadoris Nibbāna"). "*All dhammas are not-self*" (Dhp 279) **includes Nibbāna as a concept**. The unconditioned = the **cessation** of consciousness, name-and-form and the six sense-bases, **experienced**.

### Takeaway
> **Saṅkhāra (A) = breath, thought, perception-feeling: the hidden "rehearsal" behind the stage. Saṅkhāra (B) = intention in the aggregates. Nibbāna = the stilling of all formations.**

## Core teaching 2: The six sense-bases and the end of proliferation (S14)

### "The All" (Sabba Sutta)
> "*What is the All? **The eye and forms, the ear and sounds, the nose and smells, the tongue and tastes, the body and touches, the mind and mind-objects.** If anyone says 'I'll teach another All', that would be mere talk.*"

Samiddhi: "***Where there is the eye, forms, eye-consciousness and things cognisable by it, there is the world.*** *Where these are not, there is no world.*"

### Mahākoṭṭhita: the question you can't ask
Mahākoṭṭhita asks Sāriputta: "*After the six contact-bases cease without remainder, **is there anything else? Nothing else? Both? Neither?***" Sāriputta says no to all four:

> "*Whoever says so is **proliferating the unproliferated**. As far as the range of the six contact-bases goes, so far goes proliferation (*papañca*). **With the cessation of the six contact-bases, proliferation ceases.***"

**Nibbāna = the cessation of proliferation.** Asking "what's left?" is itself proliferation.

### The rope between the oxen (Koṭṭhita Sutta)
Is the eye the fetter of forms, or forms the fetter of the eye? **Neither.** A black ox and a white ox are yoked by **one rope**. The rope is the fetter, not either ox. **Desire-and-lust between them** is the fetter.

### The tortoise (Kummopama)
A tortoise feeding on a riverbank sees a jackal and **pulls its four legs and head into its shell**. The jackal waits for a limb to come out, then gives up. **Māra waits at your six doors** the same way. Guard the senses: **don't grasp at the sign or the details**.

### Moliya Phagguna: "Who touches?"
"*Not a proper question.*" Ask "*conditioned by what?*" → "*The six sense-bases condition contact; contact conditions feeling*" (Part III).

### The Mahāsaḷāyatanika Sutta (MN 149)
| Not knowing the six senses as they are | Knowing them as they are |
|---|---|
| lust arises → the five aggregates **accumulate** (*upacaya*) | they are **dismantled** (*apacaya*) |
| bodily and mental **stress, torment, fever** | bodily and mental **ease** |
| | the path factors arise; the 37 requisites of awakening are fulfilled; **calm and insight are yoked together** like a pair of oxen |

### Two ways, not contradictory
**Satipaṭṭhāna** and the **perception of impermanence** (*aniccasaññā*). Fully developed, the perception of impermanence "*removes all sensual lust, lust for form, lust for existence, ignorance, and the conceit 'I am'*". Both lead to the same place.

### Takeaway
> **The world = the six senses and their objects. When they cease, proliferation ceases: that is Nibbāna. The fetter is the "rope" of desire between sense and object, not the sense or the object.**

## Core teaching 3: The secret of contact (S15, meditation workshop)

### Upstream vs downstream
The Dhamma goes **against the stream** (*paṭisotagāmī*). There is a narrow, perverse **stream of existence** and the great river of the Dhamma flowing toward cessation. **Contact is the junction** where you choose.

> "*Those overcome by contact, following the stream of existence, on a wrong path, are far from the end of fetters. **Those who fully understand contact** and delight in peace … are fully quenched.*" (Dvayatānupassanā)

### How contact fools us
Madhupiṇḍika Sutta: "*Dependent on eye and forms, eye-consciousness arises; **the meeting of the three is contact**.*"

But consciousness is the **product** of eye and form. **Counting the product as a third thing** next to its two conditions is the trick. It creates "**me (here) seeing it (there)**". The whole perception aggregate is like a **mirage**: "*I am here, the water is over there*", yet the "water" moves when you move. (The Dutiya Dvaya Sutta adds: eye, forms and their conditions are all "*moving, tottering, impermanent, changing*".)

**Wise attention** (*yoniso manasikāra*) = attending **at the source, the point of contact**.

### Table tennis against Māra
- A clumsy player **lets the ball bounce** around (proliferation), then bends down to pick it up.
- A skilled player **returns it at the point of impact**.
- At each sense contact, Māra serves. **Return it instantly with the racket "*anicca*!"** (impermanent).

### The duel without a shield
In the "dual" (*dvandva*) of sense and object, you have only a sword, no shield. It must attack and defend at once. **Speed of attention** is everything.

### The Indriyabhāvanā Sutta (MN 152): how fast to return
When something pleasant or unpleasant arises, the noble disciple knows: "*This arose; it is conditioned, gross, dependently arisen. **This is peaceful, this is sublime: equanimity**.*" How quickly?

| Sense door | Speed of return |
|---|---|
| **Eye** | as quickly as a man with eyes **opens or shuts them** |
| **Ear** | as quickly as a **snap of the fingers** |
| **Nose** | as **water drops roll off a tilted lotus leaf** |
| **Tongue** | as one **spits out a ball of saliva** from the tip of the tongue |
| **Body** | as a strong man **bends or stretches his arm** |
| **Mind** | as **two or three drops of water fall on an iron pan** heated all day: they fall slowly, but evaporate instantly |

### Examples at each door
- **Ear, the cicada:** the cicada's "rrrr" is the **enemy of calm but the friend of insight**: it is clearly **a series of "r"s**, a *heap*, not one solid sound. *Compact perception dissolves into heap perception.*
- **Nose, the scent thief:** a deity scolds a monk for **sniffing a lotus**: "*you are stealing scent!*". (The air carries the scent to you.)
- **Tongue, honey on a razor:** savouring taste is like licking honey off a razor's edge. The Buddha "*experiences taste but not lust for taste*". Old monks at Mihintale became arahants over their morning gruel.
- **Body, the flayed cow:** a cow with patches of skin missing is bitten by creatures **wherever it stands**: by the wall, under a tree, in water, in the open. **Skin is a wrapping of wounds.** (Velvet feels good on an unwounded arm and stings on a wounded one.)
- **Mind, the computer:** **desire is the mouse** (hidden in your hand), **attention is the cursor**, **contact is the menu** that pops up. "*All things are rooted in desire, come into being through attention, arise from contact*" (Kimmūlaka).

### The raft and the relays
- The **raft** (Alagaddūpama): "*for crossing over, not for holding on*". Even good teachings are to be let go.
- **Seven relay chariots** (Rathavinīta): seven purifications, each just to reach the next.
- The author's **four-runner relay race**: the Buddha's team has four runners (the four paths). Each dies as he hands on the baton; the last touches the finishing post and dies. **Who gets the prize?** No one, and that is not annihilation: "*only suffering arises, only suffering ceases*". **Make the Buddha's team win**; don't chase "Siyadoris Nibbāna".

### Takeaway
> **Contact = counting the product (consciousness) as a third thing. Meet each contact at the point of impact with "anicca", as fast as the Indriyabhāvanā similes show.**

## Core teaching 4: Feeling, and working with pain (S16, meditation workshop)

### Six feelings, three kinds
Feeling is sixfold (born of eye-contact … mind-contact) and threefold (pleasant, painful, neutral).

### The Cūḷavedalla Sutta: what each feeling really is
The nun Dhammadinnā explained:
- **Pleasant feeling** is *pleasant while it lasts, painful when it changes*.
- **Painful feeling** is *painful while it lasts, pleasant when it changes*.
- **Neutral feeling** is *pleasant with knowledge, painful without it*.

**Latent tendencies:** lust lies in pleasant feeling, aversion in painful, ignorance in neutral. **But not always:**
- In the **first jhāna** (pleasant), there is **no** tendency to lust.
- In **grief born of longing for liberation** (painful), there is **no** tendency to aversion.
- In the **fourth jhāna** (neutral), there is **no** tendency to ignorance.

**The chain of counterparts:** pleasant ↔ painful; neutral ↔ ignorance; ignorance ↔ knowledge; knowledge ↔ liberation; liberation ↔ **Nibbāna**. The escape goes **through neutral feeling and wisdom**, not through the seesaw.

### The seesaw (Salla Sutta)
The untaught person, hit by pain, **knows no escape except sensual pleasure**, so he swings between pleasure and pain like a seesaw. He is "*struck by a second dart*" (mental pain on top of physical). Neutral feeling is hard to notice, **like a deer's tracks on a rock** (the commentary's image).

### Two sticks (Phassamūlaka Sutta)
> "*When two sticks are rubbed together, heat is produced; **when they are separated, the heat ceases**. In the same way, dependent on a contact to be felt as pleasant, pleasant feeling arises …; with the cessation of that very contact, that feeling ceases.*"

Feeling is **produced at contact** and ends **when that very contact ends**. It is **like bubbles in the rain**: each drop makes a bubble that bursts at once.

### Nandakovāda: the butcher and the hide
Ven. Nandaka taught five hundred nuns with three similes:
1. **The lamp:** the oil, wick and flame are impermanent, so how could the **light** be permanent?
2. **The tree:** root, trunk, branches are impermanent, so how could the **shadow** be permanent?
3. **The butcher:** a skilled butcher **separates the hide from the flesh** with a sharp knife, cutting the **connecting sinews** without damaging either. The **sinews = delight-and-lust**; **the knife = noble wisdom**.

On the 14th day the nuns were "**not yet full**", like the moon. After the **same sermon** on the 15th, **the least of them was a stream-enterer**. Repetition matters.

### Godhika: handle with care
The monk Godhika reached temporary liberation of mind **six times** and lost it each time through illness. The seventh time he **took his own life** and attained final Nibbāna as he died. Māra searched for his consciousness like **dark smoke** in every direction and could not find it: Godhika passed away "***with consciousness unestablished***" (*appatiṭṭhitena viññāṇena*).

> ⚠️ **The author warns strongly against reading this as approval of suicide.** It is an exceptional case; the point is **letting go of the rope** in Māra's tug-of-war **at the last moment** (like a horse winning in a photo finish), not the act itself.

### Practical advice for sitting with pain
1. **Lengthen your sitting by five minutes at a time.** Don't jump to hours.
2. Make the **fourfold determination**: "*Let only skin, sinews and bones remain, let flesh and blood dry up, I will not give up effort until I attain …*"
3. **Stay with the painful sensation** without moving, until it **cools** and the **compact perception** of "a pain" breaks into **a heap of bubbles** (tiny arising-and-ceasing sensations).
4. The author calls this "**a rough road to Nibbāna**": hard, but direct.

> ⚠️ For any real medical condition, see a doctor. The practice is about seeing feeling clearly, not about injuring the body.

### Takeaway
> **Feeling arises at contact and ends with it, like heat between two sticks. Watch it until the "solid" pain is seen as a heap of bubbles. Wisdom is the butcher's knife that cuts the sinews of delight-and-lust.**

## Common misunderstandings to avoid

| ❌ | ✅ |
|---|---|
| "Saṅkhāra always means kamma formations." | Two senses: **(A)** breath / thought / perception-feeling, **(B)** intention in the aggregates. |
| "The unconditioned is a realm that exists somewhere." | It is the **cessation** of the conditioned, experienced; not a "thing". |
| "Sense restraint means not seeing." | It means **not grasping at signs and details**, like the tortoise. |
| "Equanimity is slow suppression." | It is **speed of return** at the point of contact. |
| "Godhika shows suicide is OK for meditators." | **No.** The author explicitly rejects this reading. |
| "Pain practice = endurance contest." | Gradual (+5 min), aiming to see the pain **as a heap of bubbles**. |

## One-page recap

> **Formations:** (A) breath, thought, perception-feeling = backstage rehearsal; (B) intention in the aggregates. Their **stilling** is bliss.
>
> **The All:** the six senses and their objects. Their cessation = cessation of proliferation = Nibbāna. The fetter is the **rope**, not the oxen.
>
> **Contact:** the product (consciousness) counted as a third → "me seeing it". Return Māra's serve **at impact**: blink, finger-snap, lotus leaf, spit, arm, hot pan.
>
> **Feeling:** arises at contact like heat between sticks; neutral feeling + wisdom is the way out; cut the sinews of delight-and-lust.
>
> **Practice:** +5 minutes; fourfold determination; watch "solid pain" become **bubbles**.

**Practice for this part (2 weeks):** Each sitting, choose one sense door and train your "speed of return" using the Indriyabhāvanā simile. Keep a log. When pain comes, use the S16 method gently.
