# Part I: Foundations, the Principle and the Whirlpool

*Teaching summary of Sermons 1–4 (pp. 1–108) of Ven. Kaṭukurunde Ñāṇananda's* Paṭicca Samuppāda Dhammaya

> **Who this is for:** a beginner who wants the key ideas before reading the sermons.
> **What you will be able to do afterwards:** explain, in your own words and with one simile each, the five ideas that the rest of the book is built on.

---

## Before you start: the 12 links in plain words

Most Buddhists learn the standard list. The book assumes you know it, so here it is. Each link is the condition for the next:

| # | Pāli | Plain meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *avijjā* | ignorance (not seeing how things really are) |
| 2 | *saṅkhāra* | formations / preparations (activity done in that ignorance) |
| 3 | *viññāṇa* | consciousness (the six kinds: eye- … mind-consciousness) |
| 4 | *nāmarūpa* | name-and-form |
| 5 | *saḷāyatana* | the six sense-bases (eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind) |
| 6 | *phassa* | contact |
| 7 | *vedanā* | feeling (pleasant, painful, neither) |
| 8 | *taṇhā* | craving |
| 9 | *upādāna* | grasping |
| 10 | *bhava* | existence / becoming |
| 11 | *jāti* | birth |
| 12 | *jarāmaraṇa* | decay-and-death (with sorrow, lamentation, pain, grief, despair) |

The **arising order** (*anuloma*) runs 1 → 12: "Dependent on ignorance, formations arise …". The **cessation order** (*paṭiloma*) runs the other way: "With the complete fading away and cessation of ignorance, formations cease …".

Part I's message is that **memorising this list is not the same as understanding it.**

## Key terms for Part I

| Term | Meaning in this book |
|---|---|
| **Idappaccayatā** | "This-conditionality": *this being, that is; with the arising of this, that arises; this not being, that is not; with the ceasing of this, that ceases.* **This is the principle; the 12 links are examples of it.** |
| **Paṭicca samuppāda** | "Dependent arising": the principle *and* its illustration as the 12 links. |
| **Paṭicca samuppanna dhammā** | "Dependently arisen things": each of the 12 links is one of these. |
| **Viññāṇa** | Consciousness. Not a soul; it is always consciousness *of* something and is named after what it depends on (eye-consciousness, ear-consciousness …). |
| **Nāma** | "Name". In the suttas it means exactly **five** factors: feeling, perception, intention, contact, attention. |
| **Rūpa** (in nāmarūpa) | "Form": the *perceived* form (form as recognised by the five factors of nāma), not the physicist's matter. |
| **Aññamañña paccayatā** | Mutual (two-way) conditioning. Used here for viññāṇa ⇄ nāmarūpa. |
| **Vaṭṭa** | "Round", "cycle". The author reads it as a **whirlpool**. |
| **Yoniso manasikāra** | Wise attention: attention that goes back to the *source*. |
| **Phassa** | Contact: the meeting of sense, object and consciousness. |
| **Anidassana viññāṇa** | "Non-manifesting consciousness": the freed mind, in which name-and-form no longer appears. |

## Core teaching 1: The principle matters more than the list

### What the book says
The Udāna describes the Buddha's night of enlightenment in three watches:
1. In the **first watch** he reflected on dependent arising in the arising order.
2. In the **second** he reflected on it in the cessation order.
3. In the **third** he saw **both together**. The verse says he stood "*dispelling Māra's army, like the sun lighting up the sky*".

The author's key point is that **what the Buddha discovered was a law (*dhammatā*)**:

> *Imasmiṃ sati idaṃ hoti, imassuppādā idaṃ uppajjati;*
> *imasmiṃ asati idaṃ na hoti, imassa nirodhā idaṃ nirujjhati.*
> "This being, this is. With the arising of this, this arises. This not being, this is not. With the ceasing of this, this ceases."

Why "this … this" and not "A … B"? Because **any two neighbouring links fit the formula.** The formula is the key; the twelve links are twelve places where the key is used. That is why the Buddha could present dependent arising with fewer links (the Mahānidāna Sutta has no ignorance or formations), with different links (the Upanisa Sutta adds faith, joy, concentration …), or as a single pair (contact → feeling).

### Assaji's verse
When Upatissa (later Sāriputta) met the monk Assaji, he heard:

> *Ye dhammā hetuppabhavā, tesaṃ hetuṃ tathāgato āha …*
> "Of things that arise from a cause, the Tathāgata has told the cause …"

Upatissa became a stream-enterer just from hearing it. The author's point: **the "cause" (hetu) here is not ignorance as some first cause. It is the law itself.** What a stream-enterer sees is:

> *Yaṃ kiñci samudayadhammaṃ, sabbaṃ taṃ nirodhadhammaṃ.*
> "Whatever has the nature to arise, all that has the nature to cease."

### "Cessation is instantaneous"
The cessation formula says "*avijjāya tveva asesavirāganirodhā …*". The small word *tveva* ("but, with the very…") matters. When ignorance ceases, **everything ceases together**, the way switching on a light ends a whole shadow-play at once. It is not a story stretched over three lives (see core teaching 5).

### The doubts that are dispelled
The verse says the Buddha's **doubts vanished**. The author links this to the **16 doubts** of the Sabbāsava Sutta: "Was I in the past? What was I? Will I be in the future? … Am I? Am I not? Where did this being come from? Where will it go?" All of them assume a self moving through time. Seeing the principle removes the ground they stand on.

### Takeaway
> **Don't ask "What was the first cause?" Ask "What is this depending on right now?"**

## Core teaching 2: Consciousness and name-and-form depend on each other (the whirlpool)

This is **the most important idea in the whole book.**

### What the suttas say
In the **Mahāpadāna Sutta**, the Bodhisatta Vipassī asks, "*Dependent on what is decay-and-death?*" and traces it back link by link: birth, existence, grasping, craving, feeling, contact, the six sense-bases, name-and-form, consciousness. Then something unexpected happens:

> *Paccudāvattati kho idaṃ viññāṇaṃ nāmarūpamhā, nāparaṃ gacchati.*
> "This consciousness **turns back** from name-and-form; it does not go beyond."

Consciousness depends on name-and-form, and name-and-form depends on consciousness. The search ends in a **loop**, not at a first cause.

The **Mahānidāna Sutta** puts the same point in a famous passage that the author quotes again and again:

> "*In so far only, Ānanda, can one be born, decay, die, pass away or be reborn; in so far only is there a pathway for designation, for language, for concepts; in so far only is the sphere of wisdom; in so far only **does the round go round** for the manifestation of a 'this-ness': that is, **name-and-form together with consciousness**.*"

### The whirlpool simile
Picture a river flowing downstream.
- A small current starts to **go against the flow**.
- The main river **pushes it back**, it pushes again, and it starts to **spin**.
- The spinning creates a **hollow (abyss) underneath** and a **pull** at the surface that sucks in leaves and sticks.
- Now the whirlpool looks like **a "thing" with a centre**: it has a "here" and a "there", and it seems to *exist* in the river.

But a whirlpool is **only water going round**. Nothing is there apart from the circling. In the same way:
- **Consciousness** and **name-and-form** keep "going round" each other.
- Out of this circling appear a **centre** ("I, here") and a **world** ("things, out there").
- **Ignorance** is not seeing that this is just circling. **Formations** are the effort that keeps it spinning.
- When the circling stops, nothing is "destroyed": the river just flows on. That is **cessation**.

### The two sheaves of reeds (S4, Naḷakalāpa Sutta)
Sāriputta gives a simple image:

> Two sheaves of reeds stand **leaning against each other**. Pull one away and the other falls; pull the other and the first falls.

Consciousness and name-and-form are like that: **neither stands on its own**. Reeds are also **hollow**: there is no core, which points to *anattā* (not-self). The author notes this image was put on the cover of the first published volume.

### A riddle to remember
> Ask consciousness "*Who are you?*" and it says, "*Ask name-and-form.*"
> Ask name-and-form "*Who are you?*" and it says, "*Ask consciousness.*"

They are **not one and not two**.

### Takeaway
> **The heart of dependent arising is a two-way loop, consciousness ⇄ name-and-form. Seeing it as a loop (not as "me" and "world") is wisdom; not seeing it is ignorance.**

## Core teaching 3: What "name" (nāma) and "form" (rūpa) really mean (the five fingers)

### Nāma = five factors
The suttas (for example the Sammādiṭṭhi Sutta) define *nāma* as:

> **vedanā** (feeling), **saññā** (perception), **cetanā** (intention), **phassa** (contact), **manasikāra** (attention).

**Consciousness is *not* on this list.** The author stresses this, because later commentaries made *nāma* mean "the four mental aggregates" *including* consciousness, which makes the two-way loop meaningless.

### The five-finger mnemonic (S2)
Hold up your hand:

| Finger | Factor | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Little finger | **Feeling** | small but mischievous: it starts the trouble (pleasant/painful) |
| Ring finger | **Perception** | the "shapely" one: it recognises shapes, colours, "this is a cup" |
| Middle finger | **Intention** | the tallest, most wilful: "I want / I will" |
| Index finger | **Contact** | the pointing, quarrelsome one: it "touches" |
| Thumb | **Attention** | works with all the others; when the hand closes into a fist, it presses right next to contact |

Why is feeling first? Because consciousness's first job is to **know pleasant, painful or neutral** (Mahāvedalla Sutta). Without feeling, the notion of "self" has nothing to hold on to.

### Rūpa = form as recognised
*Rūpa* in name-and-form is **not "matter" as science describes it.** It is the **perception of form**: hardness (earth), cohesion (water), heat (fire), motion (air), as these are *recognised* through the five factors. The author's phrase:

> "*In nāma-rūpa, nāma is the 'name-form' (the form of naming), and rūpa is 'name-only form'.*"

Form is known only through name. That is why the two always come as a pair.

### Takeaway
> **Name = feeling, perception, intention, contact, attention (five fingers). Form = what those five recognise as "solid, wet, hot, moving". Consciousness stands opposite this pair, not inside it.**

## Core teaching 4: Contact, the root, and how "two" become three (S3)

### The riddle of the Ekamūla Sutta
A deity asks a riddle: "*One root, two whirlpools, three stains, five spreads, twelve eddies, an ocean, an abyss: who has crossed it?*" The author decodes it from other suttas:

| Riddle word | Meaning (from the suttas) |
|---|---|
| **One root** | **Contact** (Phassamūlaka Sutta: feelings are "born of contact, rooted in contact") |
| **Two whirlpools** | swinging between **pleasant and painful** feeling (Salla Sutta) |
| **Three stains** | lust, hate, delusion |
| **Five spreads** | the five strands of sensual pleasure / the five floods |
| **Twelve eddies** | the 12 sense-bases (six internal, six external) |
| **Ocean** | "*the eye is a person's ocean, forms are its waves*" (Samudda Sutta) |
| **Abyss** (*pātāla*) | **painful bodily feeling** (Pātāla Sutta) |

So the arahant who "crossed the abyss" is one whom **painful feeling no longer drowns**.

### Two kinds of contact (Mahānidāna)
- **Impact contact** (*paṭigha-samphassa*): the "form" side, the actual bump.
- **Designation contact** (*adhivacana-samphassa*): the "name" side, the *naming* of what was bumped.

Contact is the **"twin child" of name and form**.

> **Simile:** Two stones knock together: no quarrel. Two *people* bump into each other in a crowd: a quarrel starts. The physical impact is the same; the difference is **naming** ("he pushed *me*!").

### How the illusion starts
Eye and form are **two**. Dependent on them, eye-consciousness arises. But we count that **product as a third thing**, sitting between the two, and call the meeting of the three "contact". From then on it seems that "*someone*" (me) touches "*something*" (the world). (This becomes central in Part IV.)

### All views come from contact (Brahmajāla)
The Brahmajāla Sutta lists 62 speculative views (eternal self, annihilation, etc.). For every one, the Buddha says: "***tadapi phassapaccayā***" (*that too is conditioned by contact*). The Buddha didn't refute views by argument; he **showed where they come from**.

### Takeaway
> **Contact is the root. Painful feeling is the "abyss". Naming turns a mere bump into "me versus it".**

## Core teaching 5: All twelve links are here and now (not "three lives")

### The standard commentarial model
Buddhaghosa's *Visuddhimagga* spreads the 12 links over **three lifetimes**:
- **past:** ignorance, formations;
- **present:** consciousness to existence (8 links);
- **future:** birth, decay-and-death.

### The author's objections (begun in Part I, developed in S10, S13 and S20)
1. **It breaks idappaccayatā.** The formula says "*this being, that is*". If one link is in a past life and the next in this one, the direct "this being" relationship between them is lost.
2. **The Mahānidāna and Mahāpadāna Suttas leave out ignorance and formations.** That is not because those links belong to the past; it is because **not seeing the consciousness ⇄ name-and-form loop *is* ignorance, and going round in it *is* formations.**
3. **The Dhamma is "*sandiṭṭhika, akāliko*"**: visible here and now, timeless. A teaching you can only verify over three lifetimes would not be "timeless".
4. **The Titthāyatana Sutta** links dependent arising directly to the **Four Noble Truths**: the arising order *is* the truth of origin, the cessation order *is* the truth of cessation. That is something to be seen in this life.

### Important: this does *not* deny rebirth
Part I also explains rebirth (S3–S4) **without a travelling soul**:
- Consciousness "**descends**" into the mother's womb and name-and-form "takes shape" there (Mahānidāna). The first embryo is the "**mud stain**" (*kalala*) that name-and-form grasps.
- In the **Cetanā Sutta**, *whatever one intends, plans, or has as a latent tendency* becomes "**a footing for consciousness**". Where consciousness gets a footing, there is renewed existence. Where it gets no footing (**unestablished consciousness**, *appatiṭṭhita viññāṇa*), there is freedom.
- **Sāti's wrong view** (Mahātaṇhāsaṅkhaya Sutta): Sāti said "*this very same consciousness runs and wanders through saṃsāra*". The Buddha rebuked him. Consciousness is **named by its condition**, like fire is named by its fuel: a log fire, a straw fire. There is no "bare" consciousness travelling between lives.

### Takeaway
> **The 12 links describe what is happening now. Rebirth is real, but it is the same process continuing, not a soul moving house.**

## Supporting teaching A: The inner tangle and the outer tangle (S4)

From the Jaṭā Sutta ("*a tangle within, a tangle without*"):

| Tangle | Between | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| **Inner tangle** | **name ⇄ form** | form is known through naming, naming needs form (impact contact and designation contact) |
| **Outer tangle** | **name-and-form ⇄ consciousness** | the whirlpool of core teaching 2 |

The sutta's last verse says the tangle is cut "***where name and form cease without remainder, together with impact and perception of form***". That is a direct description of Nibbāna (taken up in Part II).

**The commentary** reads "inner/outer tangle" as craving for one's own and others' things. The author argues the sutta itself points to nāma, rūpa and viññāṇa.

**Simile, the monkey:** the mind is like a monkey in the forest that lets go of one branch only to grab another. That restless grab-and-release *is* consciousness swinging between name-and-form.

## Supporting teaching B: Worlds made of the 12 links

The author repeatedly shows that **the 12 links can be seen in everyday "worlds"**. These similes come back throughout the book:

- **Cinema world.** The film can only be shown when the hall is **dark** (ignorance). The actors' make-up and rehearsal (formations) make the story convincing. We enter a whole world of joys and sorrows. **When the lights come on, the whole world vanishes at once**, and nothing real was destroyed.
- **Cricket world.** Once players are divided into **two sides**, the bat, ball and players take on new names ("bowler", "wicket") and a whole drama of winning and losing appears.
- **The Zen tug-of-war.** Two friends, Ajith and Sumith, are made to play a game for life or death. As one is about to lose, the teacher **sweeps the pieces off the board**: game over, and both are freed. The "game" existed only because they grasped it.
- **The dog on the plank.** A dog crossing a narrow plank bridge sees its reflection and wags, growls or stares. It **sees "another dog" because it looks**: it doesn't look because there is another dog.
- **Narcissus.** He fell in love with his own reflection, thinking it was a water-nymph. If he had realised "*I see it only because I look*", he would have been free.
- **The gem in the pond** (Mahosadha). Everyone tries to drain the pond to get the gem. The wise one looks **up** and sees the gem is in a crow's nest on a palm tree; the pond only reflects it.

All these say the same thing: **the "world" is real *as experience*, but its seeming independence comes from not seeing what it depends on.**

## Supporting teaching C: Attention is the "crown witness"

In a court case without witnesses, the prosecution sometimes **pardons one of the accused** so that he will testify against the others. In the same way, the Buddha "pardoned" **attention** (*manasikāra*, the thumb) out of the gang of five name-factors.

- Until now, attention has been **unwise** (*ayoniso*): it went along with the others in making a world.
- Now it is asked to **testify truthfully**: to see feeling *as* feeling, perception *as* perception, intention *as* intention.
- That is **wise attention (*yoniso manasikāra*)**: attention that goes back to the **source**, to contact itself.

The Kimmūlaka Sutta: "*All things are rooted in desire, come into being through attention, arise from contact …*". **Attention is the biggest thief, and also the one who can turn witness.**

## Common misunderstandings to avoid

| ❌ Misunderstanding | ✅ What Part I actually says |
|---|---|
| "Dependent arising = memorising 12 links." | It is a **principle** (idappaccayatā); the links are examples. |
| "Ignorance is the first cause of everything." | There is no first cause; the search ends in a **loop**. The "cause" in Assaji's verse is the **law**. |
| "Nāma means the mind, including consciousness." | Nāma = **five factors**; consciousness is its *partner*, not part of it. |
| "Rūpa means physical matter." | Rūpa = **form as perceived**; it is known only through nāma. |
| "This teaching denies rebirth." | It explains rebirth **without a soul**: consciousness finds a footing through craving and intention. |
| "Cessation means annihilation." | Only the **whirlpool** stops; the river was never the problem. |

## One-page recap

> **The principle:** *This being, that is; this ceasing, that ceases.* The 12 links are examples.
>
> **The centre:** consciousness ⇄ name-and-form, a **whirlpool** / **two sheaves of reeds**.
>
> **Name** = feeling, perception, intention, contact, attention (**five fingers**). **Form** = form as perceived.
>
> **Root** = contact. **Abyss** = painful feeling. Naming turns a bump into a quarrel.
>
> **Ignorance** = not seeing the loop. **Formations** = keeping it spinning. **Cessation** = the whirlpool stops, here and now.
>
> **Rebirth** = consciousness finding a new footing through craving and intention, not a soul travelling.
>
> **The way in** = **wise attention**, the thumb that turns witness.

**Practice for this part:** Several times a day, notice one experience and ask: "*What is this depending on right now?*" In meditation, when the mind wanders, ask "*Which finger moved first?*"
