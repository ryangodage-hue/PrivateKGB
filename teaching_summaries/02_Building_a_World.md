# Part II: How the Whirlpool Builds a "World"

*Teaching summary of Sermons 5–8 (pp. 109–216)*

> **Builds on Part I:** the whirlpool (consciousness ⇄ name-and-form), the five fingers, contact as root.
> **Part II answers:** How does that whirlpool produce *things*, an *I*, and a *world*? And what is it like when it stops?

---

## Key terms for Part II

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Akkheyya** | "The nameable": what can be spoken of. In S5 it means name-and-form. |
| **Vohāra / sammuti / paññatti** | Convention, common usage, concept: the shared language of the world. |
| **Maññanā / maññita** | **Conceiving / a conceit**: the mind's habit of turning experience into "a thing" and relating "me" to it. |
| **Dvayatā** | **Duality / a pair**: seeing two (here/there, is/is not, self/world). |
| **Atthitā / natthitā** | "Is-ness / is-not-ness": the two extremes of existence and non-existence. |
| **Majjhimā paṭipadā** / **majjhena** | The Middle (way). |
| **Itthabhāva / aññathābhāva** | **"Thisness" / "otherwiseness"**: something becoming "this" and then changing into "other". |
| **Papañca** | Proliferation: the mind's run-away elaboration of concepts. |
| **Saḷāyatana-nirodha** | **Cessation of the six sense-bases**: the author's key description of Nibbāna. |
| **Diṭṭheva dhamme** | "Here and now", in this very life. |

## Core teaching 1: Language is built on the whirlpool (S5)

### The Samiddhi Sutta verse
A deity tempts the young monk Samiddhi to enjoy sense pleasures first and practise later. Samiddhi replies that sense pleasures are "**time-consuming**" (*kālika*), while the Dhamma is "**visible here and now, timeless, come-and-see**". The Buddha then gives a verse:

> *Akkheyyasaññino sattā, akkheyyasmiṃ patiṭṭhitā;*
> *akkheyyaṃ apariññāya, yogamāyanti maccuno.*
> "Beings who perceive in terms of the nameable are **established on the nameable**. Not fully understanding the nameable, they come under the yoke of Death."
>
> *Akkheyyañca pariññāya, akkhātāraṃ na maññati …*
> "Having fully understood the nameable, **one does not imagine a 'speaker'** …"

The "nameable" is **name-and-form**. We live inside a world of names and stand on it as if it were solid ground.

### Perception ripens into speech
From the Nibbedhika Sutta:

> *Vohāravepakkaṃ ahaṃ bhikkhave saññaṃ vadāmi.*
> "Monks, I say that **perception has expression (speech) as its result.** As one perceives a thing, so one expresses it: 'I was percipient thus.'"

Perception (the ring finger) **naturally turns into words**. Words then firm up the "things" perceived.

### Grammar hides a self
Language has **first, second and third persons**: "I am", "you are", "he is". It also has "here" and "there". The author argues this **framework grows out of the conceit "I am"** (*asmimāna*), which in turn grows out of the whirlpool. **Language is a product of ignorance and craving**, and it then hardens them.

### But the arahant still talks
The Arahanta Sutta: the arahant "*knowing the world's conventions, uses them **merely as expressions***". The problem is **not using language. It is being fooled by it.** The Buddha himself said "I" and "you", but without grasping. (S11 develops this with the milk → curd → butter → ghee simile.)

### Similes
- **The dying rich man** clings to his "shadow" (his name and possessions) and is reborn as a rat-snake or a frog by his own chair.
- **The magic kettle:** water seems to pour endlessly, but a hidden tube keeps refilling it. The "world" seems self-sustaining until you see the hidden tube.

### Takeaway
> **Name-and-form produces names; names produce "things"; "things" seem to need an owner, "I". Wisdom sees language as convention, useful but empty.**

## Core teaching 2: Dependent arising *is* the Middle (S5–S6)

### The Kaccānagotta Sutta (key sutta, read it in full)
Kaccānagotta asks: "*What is right view?*" The Buddha:

> "*This world, Kaccāna, mostly relies on a duality: **'it exists' and 'it does not exist'.***
> *For one who sees with right wisdom the **arising** of the world as it is, there is no 'it does not exist' about the world.*
> *For one who sees with right wisdom the **cessation** of the world as it is, there is no 'it exists' about the world.*
> *'Everything exists': this is one extreme. 'Nothing exists': this is the other extreme.*
> ***Without going to either extreme, the Tathāgata teaches the Dhamma by the middle:*** *dependent on ignorance, formations …*"

### Why this matters
1. Most Buddhists learn only one "middle way": the path between **sensual indulgence and self-torment** (from the first sermon). The author shows there is a **deeper middle**: **between "is" and "is not"**. And that middle **is dependent arising itself**.
2. The first sermon was framed for five ascetics who were caught in the indulgence/self-torment problem. The Kaccānagotta middle addresses the **philosophical root** of all views.
3. The commentaries largely **neglected this second middle**. That, says the author, is why the deep suttas on Nibbāna were later misunderstood.

### "Nothing is lost"
What ceases in Nibbāna is only **the ignorance that thought "something truly exists"** and **the craving that thought "it is not enough"**. Seeing the world *arise*, you can't say "nothing exists". Seeing it *cease*, you can't say "something permanently exists". **This is why Nibbāna is not annihilation.**

### The flame that went out (S5)
To Upasīva the Buddha said:

> "*As a flame blown out by the wind goes to its end and **cannot be reckoned**, so the sage, freed from name-and-body, goes to the end and cannot be reckoned … **all pathways of speech are cut off**.*"

The flame did not go "somewhere". The question "where did it go?" is itself built on the dual "is/is not" logic. Aśvaghoṣa's verse makes the same point: a lamp that goes out goes neither to earth nor sky, but by exhaustion of oil, only to peace.

### Takeaway
> **The world is neither "really there" nor "not there"; it arises and ceases dependently. Seeing that is the Middle, and it removes both eternalism and annihilationism.**

## Core teaching 3: Saṃsāra is the journey of ignorance itself (S6)

### The Dvayatānupassanā Sutta
**This sutta supplies the verses for many of the later sermons (S6–S8, S13, S15–S19), so get to know it.** On a full-moon night the Buddha taught **contemplations in pairs (dyads)**: "*Whatever suffering arises, all of it is due to X. With the cessation of X, there is no arising of suffering.*" He applied this to ignorance, formations, consciousness, contact, feeling, craving, grasping, nutriment, and so on. **Sixty monks became arahants.**

The ignorance dyad says:

> *Jātimaraṇasaṃsāraṃ, ye vajanti punappunaṃ,*
> *itthabhāvaññathābhāvaṃ, avijjāyeva sā gati.*
> "Those who go again and again through saṃsāra's birth and death, to **thisness and otherwiseness**: **that journey is ignorance itself.**"

### "Thisness" and "otherwiseness"
- Whenever something becomes "**this**" (a thing, a self, a possession), it **immediately begins to become "other"**: it changes, decays, is lost.
- **Birth** is "thisness"; **decay and death** are "otherwiseness". They are **one pair**. Even growth *is* decay.
- Lokavolokana (Udāna): "*Whatever they conceive it as, it **becomes otherwise** than that.*"

So saṃsāra is not a person travelling. It is **ignorance travelling**, between "this" and "other".

### The snake-circle cartoon
A cartoon shows a cobra and a viper, each swallowing the other's tail, until (in the last panel) **nothing is left**. Logically the last panel is "correct", but in life we are **stuck in the middle panel**: budget deficits, rights and duties, breathing in and out, the blood's circulation. **Each depends on the other**, and the circle never ends by itself.

### Siyadoris's death
Siyadoris Appu died. Why?
- The monk said he broke the fifth precept.
- The school principal said illiteracy.
- The politician said poverty.
- His wife said he couldn't breathe.
- The coroner said alcohol.
- **The Buddha would say: because he was born.**

**Wise attention begins at the end** (decay-and-death) and traces back, like the Bodhisatta Vipassī.

### Takeaway
> **No one is travelling through saṃsāra; ignorance is the traveller. Every "this" carries its "other".**

## Core teaching 4: "In the seen, just the seen" (S6, Bāhiya)

Bāhiya Dārucīriya begged the Buddha for a teaching while the Buddha was on his alms-round. The Buddha said:

> "*In the seen there will be just the seen; in the heard, just the heard; in the sensed, just the sensed; in the cognised, just the cognised.*
> *When this is so for you, Bāhiya, then **you will not be 'by that'**; when you are not 'by that', **you will not be 'in that'**; when you are not 'in that', **you will be neither here nor beyond nor in between**. This itself is the end of suffering.*"

Bāhiya became an arahant on the spot.

**The author's explanation:** "*by that*" and "*in that*" are **the grammar of seeing a "thing"**. You sit "*by*" the chair, the chair is "*in*" the room. If the seen is **just the seen** (an event, not "a thing seen by me"), the grammar collapses: no "here" (me), no "there" (it), and no "in between" (consciousness as a middle).

Related verse (Udāna): "*For the one who leans, there is wavering; for the one who does not lean, there is no wavering … no coming and going … neither here nor beyond.*"

### Takeaway
> **Experience becomes a "thing" through a hidden grammar of "by", "in", "here", "there". Seeing it bare dissolves the grammar, and with it the self that was standing in it.**

## Core teaching 5: Conceiving, the subtlest bond (S6–S7)

### Vepacitti's bonds
In the Yavakalāpi Sutta, the Asura king Vepacitti is **bound or freed by his own thoughts**. The Buddha says: "*Subtler still is Māra's bond. **Conceiving, one is bound by Māra; not conceiving, one is freed.***"

The **nine conceits** (*maññita*): "**I am**", "I am this", "I shall be", "I shall not be", "I shall be with form", "formless", "percipient", "non-percipient", "neither". These are called a **disease, a boil, a dart**.

### The Mūlapariyāya Sutta (MN 1): the "ABC of the Dhamma"
This is the first sutta of the Majjhima Nikāya. Teachers often skip it because "*even the monks who heard it didn't rejoice*". The author calls it **the ABC of the whole Dhamma**.

It lists **24 concepts**: earth, water, fire, air; beings, gods, Brahmā …; the formless spheres; the seen, heard, sensed, cognised; unity, diversity, **the All**, and **Nibbāna**. It then describes **three attitudes**:

| Who | Attitude toward "earth" (and each concept) |
|---|---|
| **The worldling** | *perceives* earth as earth → **conceives** earth, conceives "in" earth, "from" earth, "earth is mine" → **delights** in earth. *Not fully understood.* |
| **The trainee** (*sekha*) | *directly knows* earth as earth → "***let him not conceive***" earth … (*mā maññi*). The training is to stop. |
| **The arahant / Tathāgata** | directly knows earth as earth → **does not conceive** … does not delight. "*Delight is the root of suffering.*" |

**Even "Nibbāna" is on the list.** If you conceive Nibbāna as "a thing", even that blocks Nibbāna. (The commentary says this "Nibbāna" means other sects' Nibbāna; the author disagrees.)

### Ānanda's arahantship (S7)
On the night before the First Council, Ānanda tried hard to become an arahant. He had made Nibbāna into **a certificate to obtain**, a "thing", which caused restlessness. Exhausted, he decided to lie down. **In the gap between sitting and lying, before his head touched the pillow, he let go** and became an arahant.

### Khemaka's scent (S7)
The monk Khemaka did not see any aggregate as "self", yet a subtle "**I am**" remained, **like the scent of a lotus**. It is not in the petals, colour or pollen, but it is there. Or like a **washed cloth** that still smells of soap until it is put in a scent-box. Hearing this, Khemaka and sixty monks became arahants.

### The blindfolded walker
Imagine a man walking blindfold toward a wall on which is written "*Nibbānaṃ paramaṃ sukhaṃ*" (Nibbāna is the highest bliss). When he reaches the wall and wants to go "one step further", he must **turn back**. The goal was never "out there". Wise attention means **turning back to the source**.

### Takeaway
> **Perceiving is fine; conceiving ("this is a thing, related to me") is the bond. Training = "let him not conceive". Even the goal must not become a thing.**

## Core teaching 6: Nibbāna is the cessation of the six sense-bases (S8)

This is **the climax of Part II** and one of the book's central claims.

### The 16th dyad
The world calls the six sense objects "pleasant", and their cessation "painful". **The noble ones see the reverse:** the **cessation of identity** (*sakkāyassuparodhana*) is bliss. "*Near at hand, yet unseen.*"

### The evidence

| Sutta | What it says |
|---|---|
| **Kāmaguṇa Sutta** | "*That sphere should be known **where the eye ceases and perception of form fades away** … where the mind ceases and perception of mind-objects fades away.*" Ānanda explains: "*The Blessed One was speaking of **the cessation of the six sense-bases**.*" (Even the commentary agrees.) |
| **Udāna (Nibbāna Sutta 1)** | "*There is that sphere where there is **no earth, no water, no fire, no air**, … not this world nor another world, not both, neither sun nor moon … **without support, without occurrence, without object**.*" Scholars read it as a distant world. The author asks: **if the mind and mind-objects have ceased, where would "sun and moon" be?** It describes an *experience*, not a place. |
| **Rohitassa Sutta** | A deity walked for a hundred years (each stride from ocean to ocean) to reach "the end of the world", and died on the way. The Buddha: "***In this very fathom-long body, with its perception and mind, I declare the world, its arising, its cessation, and the path.***" |
| **Lokantagamana** | Ānanda: "*That **by which** one perceives the world and conceives the world is called 'the world' in the noble discipline: the eye … the mind.*" |

### The gourd folktale
A village headman hires an exorcist to do a devil-dance **inside a gourd**. The gourd is swept down a canal into the river, out to sea, and swallowed by a shark. Fishermen catch the shark and cut it open in a wedding kitchen, and **the drumming is still going on inside!**

**Every philosopher and scientist is drumming inside the "gourd" of the six sense-bases.** A rocket can't fly out of it, any more than Rohitassa could walk out of it.

### The fisherman's net (Brahmajāla)
A fisherman casts a fine net over a pond. Every fish that rises or dives is **inside the net**. All 62 views, however lofty, are "*felt by repeatedly touching through the six sense-bases*": they are **caught in the mesh of contact**. The way out: knowing the arising, passing away, gratification, danger and escape of the six sense-bases.

### "Siyadoris Nibbāna"
The author mocks the idea of Nibbāna found in obituary notices ("*May he attain the eternal bliss of Nibbāna*"), as though Nibbāna were a pleasant heavenly realm one goes to after death. He calls this "**Siyadoris Nibbāna**". The Buddha's Nibbāna is **the cessation of existence, realised in this life**.

### Uṇṇābha's chain of refuges
Each of the five sense faculties has its own domain. Their refuge is **mind**; mind's refuge is **mindfulness**; mindfulness's refuge is **liberation**; liberation's refuge is **Nibbāna**. **To ask beyond that is to overreach.** That is why mindfulness (*satipaṭṭhāna*) is "the one way".

### Takeaway
> **The "world" = the six sense-bases. The "end of the world" = their cessation, experienced in this fathom-long body. That is Nibbāna, not a distant place or an after-death heaven.**

## Common misunderstandings to avoid

| ❌ Misunderstanding | ✅ Part II says |
|---|---|
| "Buddhism says nothing really exists." | That is one **extreme**. Dependent arising is the **Middle**. |
| "Using the word 'I' is wrong." | Arahants use it **as convention**; the problem is grasping. |
| "Nibbāna is a blissful realm after death." | That is "**Siyadoris Nibbāna**". Nibbāna = cessation of the six sense-bases, here and now. |
| "Wanting Nibbāna is fine as long as I 'get' it." | Making Nibbāna a **thing to get** blocks it (Ānanda's story). (Part V looks at "desire for Nibbāna" more closely.) |
| "Nibbāna = annihilation." | Only ignorance and craving cease; there was **no self** to annihilate. |

4. List the three attitudes of the Mūlapariyāya Sutta.
5. What did Ānanda do wrong before his arahantship, and what happened at the "posture junction"?
6. Give two suttas that support "Nibbāna = cessation of the six sense-bases".
7. What is "Siyadoris Nibbāna"?

<summary><b>Answers</b></summary>

1. "Everything exists" and "nothing exists"; the middle is dependent arising (seeing arising and cessation).
2. The moment something becomes "this" (a new phone that is "mine"), it begins to become "other" (scratched, outdated, lost).
3. The grammar of treating the seen as "a thing" related to a self ("seen *by* me", "I am *in* it").
4. Worldling: conceives and delights; trainee: "let him not conceive"; arahant: does not conceive or delight.

## One-page recap

> **Names:** the whirlpool produces names; perception ripens into speech; grammar quietly builds a self.
>
> **The Middle:** not "everything is" nor "nothing is", but **dependent arising** (Kaccānagotta).
>
> **Saṃsāra:** ignorance's journey between **thisness** and **otherwiseness**.
>
> **Bāhiya:** in the seen, just the seen → not "by that", not "in that" → neither here nor there.
>
> **Conceiving:** the subtlest bond. Worldling conceives; trainee is told "don't"; arahant doesn't. Even "Nibbāna" can be conceived.
>
> **Nibbāna:** the **cessation of the six sense-bases**, "in this fathom-long body", **not** "Siyadoris Nibbāna".

**Practice for this part:** For a day, add "*by convention*" whenever you think "I am …". In meditation, look at one sound "as just heard", without a "hearer" and without "a sound out there".
